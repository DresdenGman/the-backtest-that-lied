\subsection{Within-Quintile Selection}
The model demonstrated genuine stock-selection ability within the lowest-liquidity quintile: Q1 holdings outperformed the Q1 equal-weight benchmark by \QOneExcessPct{} per month. This excess is not explainable by the size, value, or momentum factors because those factors capture \textit{between}-quintile variation; within-quintile excess represents stock-specific selection.

The within-quintile excess decays monotonically across quintiles:
Q2 excess was negative ($-2.8\%$ monthly), indicating the model selected the \textit{wrong} stocks in the middle-liquidity range. Q3--Q5 showed weak or negative selection effects.

\subsection{Interpretation}
The signal behaves as a low-liquidity stock selector. This is consistent with findings by Leippold, Wang, and Zhou (2022), who document that short-horizon machine-learning predictability in Chinese equities is strongest among the smallest, least liquid stocks. The finding does not, however, constitute a ``size effect'' in the Fama-French sense, because true point-in-time market capitalization was unavailable. The ADV20-based finding should be interpreted as a liquidity effect, not a size-factor finding.

\subsection{Scalability Barrier}
The concentration in Q1 creates a fundamental capacity constraint. With median daily turnover of \textyen 19M per stock and a typical position of 1--2\% of ADV20, the strategy could deploy approximately \textyen 5--10M before market impact became material. This is a research-level capacity, not an institutional investment capacity.

The strategy's dependence on the lowest-liquidity segment also means that returns are subject to execution risk: the stocks most critical to performance are precisely those where trading costs are highest and price impact is most severe. The cost model used here (flat 31bp round-trip) likely underestimates true costs in Q1, meaning the reported net returns for the full-universe portfolio are upper bounds rather than achievable outcomes.
