\subsection{The Excess-Return Trap}
A common error in strategy evaluation is to report only benchmark-relative excess. Table~\ref{tab:portfolio} illustrates the problem: U50 RESID generates +4.9\% annual excess over a declining benchmark, but an investor allocating capital to the strategy would still lose money in absolute terms.

Positive excess over a poor benchmark is not evidence of investment skill. The correct comparison is between the strategy's absolute return and the investor's opportunity cost (risk-free rate plus appropriate risk premium). An equity strategy that returns \ULiquidLoss{} annually while the risk-free rate is positive destroys value, regardless of how the benchmark performs.

\subsection{Cost Analysis}
Transaction costs accounted for approximately 4.2 percentage points of annual return drag in the U50 portfolio. The primary cost drivers were:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{Turnover:} Monthly one-way turnover of approximately 53\% in U50 and 51\% in U100, consistent with a 90/80 band applied to a volatile ranking signal.
    \item \textbf{Stamp tax:} 5bp on all sells (the largest single cost component at roughly 3.2\% p.a.).
    \item \textbf{Commission and slippage:} Combined 6bp per round trip (approximately 1.0\% p.a.).
\end{itemize}

Cost reduction through wider bands would reduce turnover but at the expense of signal decay---stocks that drop in rank would be held longer, diluting the active return. The cost-turnover trade-off was not optimized because the gross signal in the liquid universe was insufficient to justify further tuning.
